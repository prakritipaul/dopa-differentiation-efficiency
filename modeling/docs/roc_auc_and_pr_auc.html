Reading the failure detector

What ROC-AUC and PR-AUC actually measure, and what “100% precision at 65% recall” buys you in cell lines and culture days.

Precision and recall are about one class

For failure detection the “positive” class is failure — the 42 lines out of 138 that never reach 20% DA/Sert neurons.

Every line falls into one of four boxes. These are the counts at the operating point discussed below:

model flagged itmodel let it pass
truly failed 27TP · true positive 15FN · false negative
truly succeeded 0FP · false positive 96TN · true negative
Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 27 / (27 + 0) = 1.000 Of everything I flagged, how much was right? Punished by false positives — good lines wrongly condemned.
Recall = TP / (TP + FN) = 27 / (27 + 15) = 0.643 Of everything I should have caught, how much did I? Punished by false negatives — bad lines that slipped through. Also called sensitivity, or the true-positive rate.
False-positive rate = FP / (FP + TN) = 0 / (0 + 96) = 0.000 The ROC curve's x-axis. Of all the genuinely good lines, how many did I wrongly flag?

Notice which box each metric ignores. Precision and recall never touch TN — the 96 successes left alone simply do not appear in either formula. The false-positive rate does use TN, and puts it in the denominator. That single difference is the whole reason ROC and PR disagree on imbalanced data: with 96 true negatives available, false positives get diluted in ROC and stay undiluted in precision.

The two trade against each other, and you choose where you sit on that trade by moving a threshold.

The threshold is a dial, not a fixed thing

The model outputs a probability per line. Flag it if P(failure) ≥ t.

Low t — flag freely

Catch nearly every failure (high recall), but sweep in good lines too (low precision).

High t — flag only the sure things

Almost no false alarms (high precision), but borderline failures slip past (low recall).

One model, infinitely many operating points. ROC-AUC and PR-AUC summarise the model across all of them at once — which is why they are threshold-free, and why neither tells you which threshold to use.

ROC-AUC — is the ranking right?

ROC-AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive scores higher than a randomly chosen negative.

At 0.961: pick one success and one failure at random, and the model gives the success the higher probability 96% of the time. It measures ordering, not calibration — whether the ranking is right, not whether the numbers are accurate. 0.5 is a coin flip; 1.0 is perfect separation.

Its blind spot, straight from the formulas above: FPR divides by FP + TN, and TN is large. Wrongly flagging 10 successes moves FPR by only 10/96 ≈ 0.10 — barely a wobble on the curve. The same 10 mistakes drop precision from 1.00 to 27/37 = 0.73, because precision has no TN to hide behind.

PR-AUC — and why its baseline moves

Same thresholds, but plotting precision against recall. It ignores true negatives entirely, so false alarms cannot hide behind a large negative class.

Its baseline is not 0.5 — it is the prevalence. A random guesser scores roughly the fraction of positives:

positive classPR-AUCrandom baselinelift
success0.9770.696+0.28
failure0.9440.304+0.64

So the failure PR-AUC of 0.944 is the more impressive number despite being the smaller one — it clears a 0.304 baseline instead of a 0.696 one. Always read PR-AUC against prevalence: 0.944 would be poor if 90% of lines failed.

Rule of thumb. ROC-AUC for “is the overall ranking good”; PR-AUC when you care about a minority class and false alarms are expensive.

“100% precision at 65% recall”

This names one operating point on that curve, not a property of the model:

threshold: flag a line if P(failure) ≥ 0.596

lines flagged            27
of those, truly failures  27   →  precision 100%
failures caught       27 / 42  →  recall 64.3%
failures missed           15

All 138 lines, one dot each

flagged & truly failed — 27 failed, missed — 15 succeeded — 96

Precision 100% — no ringed dot is blue. Not one good line was wrongly condemned. Recall 65% — 15 orange dots carry no ring: real failures the model let through.

In practice: bin those 27 lines with complete confidence and save 41 days of culture on each. The other 15 bad lines will waste your time regardless.

Why stop at 65%?

Because the trade turns brutal at the end:

recallprecisionfailures caughtgood lines lost
50%1.00021 of 420
65%0.96827 of 42~1
80%0.92134 of 42~3
90%0.86438 of 42~6
100%0.37542 of 42~70

Catching every failure means flagging about 112 lines to find 42 — discarding roughly 70 perfectly good ones. 65% is where the “never discard a good line” guarantee runs out.

Which point you want depends on your costs. If a wasted differentiation run is expensive and a replacement line is cheap, push recall higher and accept the false alarms. The metric does not make that call — you do.
